== abstract
Named Data Networking (NDN) retrieves named content in a receiver-driven way, which breaks the loss-driven assumptions of TCP congestion control and prevents direct reuse of data-centre schemes such as DCQCN or HPCC. This work develops an NDN congestion-management framework combining router-side EWMA queue monitoring with an adaptive statistical marking threshold, a consumer-side PID Interest-window law driven by Data-carried marks and the per-Interest queuing delay that the one-Data-per-Interest invariant makes unambiguous, and a residual LSTM forecaster that can raise proactive marks. A replicated discrete-event study of 2,210 runs (10 seeds per configuration) covered three topologies, six scenarios with injected failures, load surges and Interest floods, and six control laws, including a PCON-style scheme and an oracle static window. In normal operation the PID law held the mean bottleneck queue at 3.0–3.6 packets, 45–90% below AIMD and 16–27% below PCON-style, raised goodput over AIMD by 13.4% and 36.0% on two topologies (−4.9% on the multipath one), and incurred no timeouts outside failures; the oracle window gained 1–13% goodput but with 1.9–5.3× larger queues. The LSTM cut forecast error by 15–54% versus persistence on dynamic queues, but an ablation showed no control benefit. Interest floods exhausted PIT state and cut legitimate goodput of all adaptive laws by 67–98%; PIT fair-share admission restored it on two topologies but not when attackers share faces with consumers. Of 270 pairwise comparisons, 203 were significant after Holm correction.

== r41
Figure 3 shows the instantaneous queue, its EWMA and the adaptive threshold of Eq. (2) on the T1 bottleneck face for a PID run and an AIMD run. Three observations follow. First, the EWMA suppresses sample-to-sample fluctuations of the instantaneous queue while following its sub-second excursions. Second, under PID the regulated queue (mean 3.1 packets) is stationary and has small variance, so μ + κσ is far below the floor and the threshold rests at θmin = 21 packets. The detector therefore never marked during PID operation in any normal-operation run: the PID loop is held in place by its delay term (Eq. (13)), and the marking path acts as a safety net. Third, under AIMD the threshold rises with the sawtooth variance (up to the ceiling θmax = 48 packets), which is the self-scaling behaviour that removes manual threshold re-tuning. The limitation is timeliness on steep ramps: with α = 0.15 the EWMA lags AIMD's ramp, and in the seed-1 trace it crosses the self-raised threshold 0.0–0.6 s after the buffer has already reached capacity, so for AIMD-like traffic the reactive detector provides no early warning. This is exactly the regime in which the forecaster beats the persistence baseline (Section 4.7). The PCON-style law, which reacts to its own CoDel-style marks, avoids overflow altogether (Table 4).

Table 4 reports the T1 comparison; Figures 4–6 show the queue, the window and the aggregated metrics. AIMD executes its characteristic limit cycle [29]: the window ramp drives the 60-packet buffer to overflow, multiplicative decrease follows, and the cycle repeats, producing 98 timeouts per run on average. Static-16 pins a standing queue of 17.3 packets (three consumers × 16 Interests exceed the bandwidth–delay product by about this amount), and the oracle Static-BDP reduces it to 8.6 packets. The PID law converges within about one second to a regulated queue of 3.1 ± 0.2 packets (90.3% below AIMD, 63.4% below Static-BDP and 27.1% below PCON-style), consistent with the equilibrium of Eq. (13).
== r42b
These queue differences translate directly into end-to-end performance. PID goodput is 5,834 kb/s, 13.4% above AIMD and 6.5% above PCON-style, and within 1.5% of the saturating static laws. Mean RTT falls to 53.6 ms, versus 108.6 ms for AIMD (−50.6%), 62.3 ms for Static-BDP and 56.1 ms for PCON-style, and the p95 RTT is 56.0 ms against 145.6 ms for AIMD. RTT jitter is 0.12 ms against 0.35 ms for AIMD. (Jitter is the mean absolute successive RTT difference; the static laws show near-zero jitter because their RTT is constant once the standing queue forms.) Jain's index of PID is 0.982, slightly below the other laws (≥ 0.997); Section 5.3 discusses fairness-restoring extensions. PID+LSTM is identical to PID because the forecaster never crossed the 21-packet threshold (Section 4.7).
== r43
On the diamond topology the adaptive weighted strategy spreads Interests over both relays, so the aggregate bottleneck capacity is 12 Mb/s. Here the ranking changes. Static-16, whose total window of 32 Interests is below the two-path bandwidth–delay product, under-utilises the network (6,766 kb/s, U = 0.57). The oracle Static-BDP reaches 11,266 kb/s and AIMD 10,426 kb/s. PID obtains 9,919 kb/s, 4.9% below AIMD and 12.0% below Static-BDP, but with a mean queue of 3.6 packets (AIMD 23.3, Static-BDP 6.7), the lowest mean RTT among the laws that use both paths (47.3 ms versus 79.2 ms for AIMD) and zero timeouts (AIMD: 45). The goodput gap to AIMD on T2 is a genuine limitation of the delay-driven error signal under multipath. Interests sent over the two relays experience different queuing delays, and the PID controller reacts to the larger delay sample and backs off before both paths are full, the multipath RTT-ambiguity problem documented for ICP [7,8]. Per-path delay normalisation (using the minimum RTT per next hop) is a natural remedy and is left for future work.
== r44a
T3 aggregates 15 consumers onto two bottlenecks: the 6 Mb/s trunk (C1–C7) and the 50 Mb/s producer link (all consumers). Static-16 saturates the trunk queue at 57.8 packets with 323 timeouts per run, the bufferbloat signature [9]. AIMD's multiplicative decreases leave capacity unused (goodput 36,291 kb/s, 2,044 timeouts). The PID law delivers 49,356 kb/s (+36.0% over AIMD, -0.9% versus PCON-style) with a 3.0-packet mean queue, the lowest mean RTT of all laws (21.3 ms; Static-BDP 28.8 ms, PCON-style 25.6 ms) and zero timeouts (PCON-style: 476). The oracle Static-BDP achieves the highest goodput (56,571 kb/s, 12.8% above PID), because it sizes each consumer's window from global knowledge of the max-min fair rates. It does so with a 16.0-packet trunk queue and the lowest Jain index (0.537), since it gives the R2-side consumers large windows. Fairness on T3 is otherwise dominated by the heterogeneous access rates and the two bottlenecks for every law (J = 0.537–0.666).

The extended 60 s runs (Table 7) confirm that these are steady-state properties: PID goodput is 49,944 kb/s with a 2.9-packet queue and zero timeouts, with no drift, integrator wind-up or slow oscillation, while Static-16 and AIMD accumulate 952 and 6,207 timeouts respectively.

Table 8 lists the goodput of every law in all 108 configurations (Tables A1 and A2 give the mean queue and RTT). The scenarios produce clearly different operating conditions. Doubling the offered load (high traffic) raises the PID mean queue only to 4.3–4.7 packets, whereas Static-16 overflows the T1 and T3 buffers (59.5 and 57.6 packets). After the load surge at t = 8 s, the 95th-percentile queue under PID stays at 5.2–7.3 packets, against 47.4–61.0 packets for AIMD. PID recorded zero timeouts in every normal, high-traffic, load-surge and DDoS configuration (maximum 0). Its goodput ranking relative to the other laws is the same as in normal operation: above AIMD on T1 and T3, below the oracle Static-BDP, and close to PCON-style.
== r45b
Failures are now visible in every metric (Table 9, Table A3 and Figure 11). The effect depends on what the fault removes. On T2, losing relay G (link R1–G or node G) halves the path capacity; the forwarding strategy stops using the dead face and traffic is rerouted through Ub. PID goodput falls from 10,532 to 5,423 kb/s during the link failure (AIMD 5,027, Static-BDP 6,015 kb/s) and returns to 10,563 kb/s after restoration. Its recovery time to 90% of the pre-fault goodput is 1.1 s (AIMD 0.8 s, PCON-style 0.5 s, Static-BDP 0.5 s). On T1, the loss of consumer C1's access link or of consumer C3 lets the remaining consumers absorb the freed capacity: PID goodput during the fault is 5,924 kb/s against 5,998 kb/s before. On T3 the failure of router R1 disconnects seven consumers; the remaining consumers keep the producer link loaded (PID 50,230 kb/s during the fault) and every law recovers within 0.7 s. Timeouts during failures are unavoidable for Interests in flight on the failed element, but PID's remain among the lowest (255 on T3 node failure versus 2,013 for AIMD and 684 for PCON-style).
== r45c
The time series in Figure 11 show that the closed loop is not invariant to failures; it adapts to them. The window contracts when capacity disappears and recovers after restoration without oscillation or overshoot of the queue. 
== r46a
Table 10 and Figure 12 report the Interest-flood scenario, with the flood injected (≈7,500 attack Interests per attacker per run, verified from the counters). Attack Interests for non-existent names hold PIT entries for their 2 s lifetime, so each 500 Interests/s attacker keeps about 1,000 entries alive, more than the 300-entry PIT. Once the PIT is full, legitimate Interests are NACKed. The adaptive laws interpret these NACKs as congestion and shrink their windows, which compounds the denial of service. During the flood, legitimate PID goodput falls by 73% on T1, 90% on T2 and 98% on T3, and AIMD and PCON-style suffer similar losses (67–98% across the three adaptive laws). The static laws, which ignore NACKs, keep more goodput (losses of 8–85%) by continuing to re-issue Interests into the contended PIT. PID still incurs zero timeouts and the lowest queue and RTT, but congestion control by itself does not confer IFA resilience: the bottleneck during a flood is PIT state, not link capacity, and no window law can create PIT capacity.
== r46b
With PIT fair-share admission (Section 3.7), the flood is confined to the attacker's own face quota on T1 and T2. PID goodput during the flood is restored to 100% and 105% of its pre-attack level, legitimate consumers receive essentially no NACKs, and the queue and RTT benefits of the PID law are retained. The two mechanisms are therefore complementary: admission protects PIT state, and the PID loop keeps the data plane at low delay. On T3 the rule restores only 18% of pre-attack goodput, because several attackers reach the routers through consumer nodes, so attack and legitimate Interests share the same incoming faces and a per-face quota cannot separate them. Defending such compromised-edge topologies needs per-prefix or per-flow statistics, or attack classification [42–47], which the framework's per-face detector statistics could feed.
== r47a
Table 11 compares the LSTM with the persistence forecast q̂(t + 0.8 s) = q(t) on identical samples. On the dynamic, sawtooth-shaped queues produced by AIMD the LSTM reduces the forecast error by 15–54% (MAE 7.00 vs 10.41 packets on T1, 10.86 vs 23.83 on T3), so it does capture build-up dynamics 0.8 s ahead. On PID-regulated queues the absolute error is small (0.74–1.90 packets), but persistence is equally good or better (0.53–2.04 packets): a queue held at about three packets has little predictable structure beyond its current value. The closed-loop PID+LSTM values (0.75–1.95 packets) are similar. Forecast accuracy on regulated queues must therefore be judged against a naive baseline. Figure 14 shows that pre-training converges smoothly for all three topologies.
== r47b
The ablation (Table A4) isolates the closed-loop contribution of the predictive layer. With the default floor θmin = 0.35B, PID and PID+LSTM differ by less than 1% in goodput in 9 of 9 topology–scenario cells: neither the regulated queue nor its forecast approaches the threshold, so the forecaster never raises a mark. With a tighter floor (θmin = 0.1B) the forecaster does raise proactive marks and lowers the p95 queue by 4–53% and the mean RTT slightly, but at a goodput cost of 5–39%. Even with a working forecaster, extra early marks trade throughput for queue on a loop that already sits near its delay-optimal operating point. Of the 81 paired ablation comparisons, 25 differ significantly after Holm correction: 16 favour PID+LSTM (lower queue or RTT) and 9 are goodput losses; none is a goodput gain. We conclude that, for the PID loop, the LSTM is useful as a monitoring and early-warning signal (e.g., for AIMD-like legacy traffic, where it beats persistence) but adds no control value. Hypothesis H2 is supported for forecasting accuracy on dynamic queues and rejected for closed-loop benefit.
== r48
Queue samples are taken locally at each router face every Δts; no samples cross the network, so sampling adds no communication overhead, and the feedback itself is one bit in Data packets that are sent anyway. Table A5 and Figure 15 vary Δts from 0.02 to 0.5 s with the horizon fixed at 0.8 s (H = 40 to 2 steps) and a forecaster pre-trained for each interval. Forecast MAE on T1 is 0.63, 0.65 and 0.81 packets at Δts = 0.02, 0.1 and 0.5 s (load surge); it is flat up to 0.2 s and degrades at 0.5 s, when samples become too sparse to resolve sub-second dynamics. Goodput and queue are unchanged across Δts, consistent with the ablation, because the PID loop runs on per-Interest RTT samples at RTT timescales, not on router samples. This answers the timeliness concern: the control loop's information is never older than one RTT, irrespective of the predictor's sampling rate. Processing overhead was measured per run: the detector costs 43 µs per sample and the LSTM 2.3 ms per inference plus 35 ms per online fine-tuning step in the interpreted Python implementation, i.e. about 2.3% of one CPU core per monitored face at Δts = 0.1 s. Online fine-tuning lets the model track traffic changes, but in our runs it did not improve accuracy over the pre-trained model on regulated queues. These costs are upper bounds that a compiled implementation would cut by two to three orders of magnitude (Section 5.4).

== d51
The results reduce to one control-theoretic statement: AIMD estimates capacity by probing to failure, whereas the PID loop regulates a measured state. Whenever the bottleneck queue is non-empty the departure rate equals the capacity, so every packet in the standing queue beyond the first adds delay without adding throughput. AIMD's limit cycle forces the queue through its full range, so its time-average delay is set by the buffer size, and its throughput loss comes from post-overflow drain intervals in which the bottleneck idles. A static window removes cycling but must be sized for one operating point: too small and it under-utilises (Static-16 on T2), too large and it bloats the buffer (Static-16 on T1 and T3). Even the oracle Static-BDP, sized from global knowledge, carries a standing queue and cannot adapt when the operating point moves (failures, surges, floods). The PID loop settles at the dead-band equilibrium of Eq. (13), a queue of order one packet plus the oscillation of its additive step, at the left knee of the delay–throughput curve, the operating point that BBR [31] and CoDel/PIE [10,23] target in TCP/IP. It reaches this point from per-Interest delay and one-bit marks rather than bandwidth estimation. The price, visible on T2 and against Static-BDP, is that a delay-targeting loop concedes some goodput when its delay signal is noisy (multipath) or when the static oracle tolerates a larger standing queue.
== d52
Against PCON [16], the de facto NDN reference re-implemented here, the PID consumer achieves comparable goodput (-0.9% to +6.5%) with 16.4–27.5% less queue, 2.2–17.1% lower RTT and no timeouts, whereas PCON-style incurs timeouts on T3. The difference comes from PID's proportional reaction to the mark fraction and delay, compared with PCON's AIMD halving, and from its use of every Data packet as a delay sample. The hop-by-hop shapers of Wang et al. [14] and Touati et al. [15] need per-face or per-flow rate state at every router; the present scheme keeps router state at a few counters per face. ICP's delay-based window [8] loses observability under multipath, and our T2 results show that the PID law's delay term suffers from the same effect. MIRCC [17] avoids it with per-hop rate stamps and is a natural complement. The RL controller of Zheng et al. [19] and IEACC [20] report gains over AIMD-family baselines of a similar order to ours; the PID loop obtains its gains with three interpretable gains, no training in the control path, and anti-windup by construction. ACCPndn [21] places a forecaster in the control path; our ablation suggests that, for a well-damped loop, forecasting adds little and is better used for monitoring. Classical PI/PID AQM in TCP/IP [24,25,33,34] regulates router queues against a sender population it cannot command; relocating the controller to the NDN consumer gives it direct authority over the arrival process, which is why a queue of about three packets is attainable. Finally, dedicated IFA defences [42,44,45,47] and congestion control address different resources (PIT state versus link capacity). Section 4.6 shows that both are needed, and that per-face admission alone is insufficient when attackers share faces with legitimate consumers.
== d55
The limitations are concrete. (i) All results come from a discrete-event simulator with an interpreted implementation. Validation with ndnSIM/NFD forwarders and on the NDN testbed is the necessary next step, as is re-discretising the controller for kernel-forwarder timescales. (ii) The PID law's delay term is path-agnostic: it under-utilises multipath capacity (T2), which per-next-hop RTT normalisation should address. (iii) Fairness is slightly below that of AIMD in several configurations, notably when late joiners compete with established flows (T2 load surge, J = 0.684); Section 5.3 outlines remedies. (iv) The PID law treats PIT-overflow NACKs like congestion, which an Interest flood can exploit; distinguishing NACK reasons and composing with stronger IFA defences (per-prefix statistics, classifiers) is required for compromised-edge topologies such as T3. (v) The LSTM brings no control benefit for PID-regulated queues; its value as an early-warning signal for heterogeneous (AIMD-like) traffic remains to be shown in deployment. (vi) Ten seeds give adequate power for the large effects reported here but limited resolution for small differences, and the topologies, while heterogeneous, are small. (vii) Cache-pollution and poisoning attacks are out of scope.
== c1
This study designed and evaluated an adaptive congestion-management framework for Named Data Networking. It couples EWMA-based detection with self-adjusting statistical thresholds and ECN-style marking at routers, a consumer-side PID Interest-window law driven by Data-carried marks and per-Interest queuing delay, a residual LSTM queue forecaster, and optionally PIT fair-share admission. It was evaluated in 2,210 simulation runs with 10 independent seeds per configuration, three topologies, six explicitly injected scenarios and six control laws, with Holm-corrected statistical testing.
== c_demo
The PID law held the mean bottleneck queue at 3.0–3.6 packets in normal operation, 45.4–90.3% below AIMD and 16.4–27.5% below a PCON-style scheme, with the lowest mean RTT on T1 and T3 and zero retransmission timeouts in every configuration without failures.
PID goodput exceeded AIMD by 13.4% (T1) and 36.0% (T3) but was 4.9% lower on the multipath T2. It was between -0.9% and +6.5% of PCON-style and 1.3–12.8% below an oracle delay-aware static window that requires global knowledge.
Under link and node failures the loop adapted without oscillation and recovered to 90% of pre-fault goodput within 0.1–1.2 s of restoration; performance degraded in proportion to the capacity lost.
Interest floods exhausted PIT state and cut legitimate goodput of every adaptive law by 67–98%; congestion control alone did not provide IFA resilience. PIT fair-share admission restored 100–105% of pre-attack goodput where attackers have their own faces, but not in a compromised-edge topology.
The residual LSTM reduced 0.8 s forecast error by 15–54% relative to persistence on dynamic AIMD queues but not on PID-regulated queues, and the ablation showed no closed-loop benefit from proactive marking.
== c_anti
Incremental deployability: one-bit feedback in an existing NFD Data field and per-face counters at routers; the fixed-point mapping of the detector to programmable switches (Section 5.4) is a design argument that has not been implemented.
Energy and memory savings from shallower buffers and fewer retransmissions, and suitability for IoT, vehicular and edge-media ICN deployments, follow from the reduced queues and timeouts but were not measured.
The transfer of a single parameterisation across topologies of different scale was observed in simulation; fleet-wide defaults in real networks remain to be validated.
== c2
Scientifically, the results show that relocating classical PID authority to the NDN consumer, and using the one-Data-per-Interest invariant to obtain an unambiguous per-Interest delay signal, yields an operating point that loss-driven probing cannot reach and that a static window can reach only with oracle knowledge and a standing queue. Learning is best placed around the loop (monitoring, early warning) rather than in it. Future work will validate the framework on ndnSIM/NFD and the NDN testbed, add per-next-hop delay normalisation for multipath, implement the fairness extensions of Section 5.3, distinguish PIT-overflow NACKs from congestion NACKs, and combine the detector's per-face statistics with IFA classifiers.
